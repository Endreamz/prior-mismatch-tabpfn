THE PRICE-PATH PRIOR: EVERY RULE AND THE REASON FOR IT
Step 3 (step3_prior_calibration), version 2: asymmetric volatility, calibrated on separate stocks
====================================================================================================

What the prior is: a simulator of synthetic daily stock prices. Each simulated price history
is run through the real pipeline, so every training task has exactly the format of the real
tasks. Values marked "calibrated" are chosen by the search in section C; the calibrated
numbers are saved in results\pricepath_params.json after the run.


A. THE SIMULATED STOCK
----------------------

1. Volatility level
   Rule:   each simulated stock gets its own annual volatility, drawn log-normally around a
           median (spread 0.35). The median is set at the end so that the simulated median
           matches the calibration stocks' median exactly.
   Why:    stocks differ greatly in how volatile they are. The level is the only statistic
           that depends on it, and the model never sees it (inputs are standardised on each
           context), so it is matched exactly rather than searched.

2. Drift
   Rule:   a small upward trend of 0.02% a day (about 5% a year). Fixed, not calibrated.
   Why:    the typical long-run stock return. It barely matters for a volatility question.

3. Volatility clustering: asymmetric GARCH(1,1)  [NEW in version 2]
   Rule:   today's variance depends on yesterday's squared return and yesterday's variance,
           pulled towards a moving target level (reaction alpha, persistence beta,
           calibrated). The reaction is asymmetric: alpha x (1 + LEVERAGE) after a fall and
           alpha x (1 - LEVERAGE) after a rise. LEVERAGE is calibrated between 0 (symmetric,
           version 1) and 1 (no reaction to rises at all, as in GJR-GARCH).
   Why:    calm and turbulent days cluster in real markets. The asymmetry is the leverage
           effect: falls raise volatility more than rises of the same size. Version 1 was
           symmetric and missed it (the in-context coefficient on distance from the 20-day
           average was -0.31 on real stocks against -0.05 in the simulator). The average
           reaction stays alpha, so the stability of the variance process is unchanged.

4. Heavy-tailed daily moves
   Rule:   daily shocks follow a Student-t distribution (degrees of freedom calibrated),
           scaled to unit variance.
   Why:    real returns have far more extreme days than a normal distribution.

5. Volatility breaks
   Rule:   breaks arrive at random (rate per year calibrated). Each lifts log-volatility by
           a random amount (mean size calibrated, spread 0.3).
   Why:    the regime breaks that are the subject of the paper. Frequency and size are
           calibrated to the real onset rate of the causal break flag.

6. Break fading
   Rule:   each break's lift decays with a 98-trading-day half-life. Fixed.
   Why:    measured directly from real breaks, not tuned.

7. Onset shocks  [CHANGED in version 2]
   Rule:   half of all breaks start with a one-day move (size calibrated, in units of that
           day's volatility). The move is downward with probability SHOCK_DOWN (calibrated:
           0.5, 0.75 or 1; version 1 used a fair coin), and it enters the variance recursion,
           so a crash day raises the following days' volatility.
   Why:    real breaks often begin with a crash day, and crash days are mostly falls.
           Version 1 added the shock after the recursion, so it had no effect on later
           volatility, which real crashes do.

8. Sell-off drift during breaks  [NEW in version 2]
   Rule:   while a break is active, the expected daily return falls by BREAK_DRIFT x (the
           break's current log-volatility lift) daily volatilities (calibrated, 0 to 0.2).
   Why:    in real markets high-volatility spells come with falling prices. This links the
           price level to volatility, which the input "distance from the 20-day average"
           picks up.

9. Overnight gaps
   Rule:   25% of each day's variance happens between the close and the next open, outside
           that day's high-low range. Fixed by assumption.
   Why:    typical for US stocks. Added in version 1 after the first realism check showed
           the answers far too persistent.

10. Day-to-day range noise
   Rule:   each trading day's variance gets a random multiplier with mean 1 (log spread
           calibrated).
   Why:    news days, quiet days and uneven trading. Added for the same reason as the gaps.

11. Daily high and low
   Rule:   each trading day is simulated as 8 small random steps from the open that end
           exactly at the close (a Brownian bridge); the high and low are the extremes.
   Why:    the answer is built on the high-low (Parkinson) volatility, so the high and low
           must behave as on real data.


B. TURNING SIMULATED PRICES INTO TASKS
--------------------------------------

12. The real pipeline, copied unchanged
   Rule:   the same 8 inputs (5 lagged returns, 10-day change, 20-day volatility, distance
           from the 20-day average), the same answer (next 5 days' high-low volatility above
           the last 126 days' median) and the same causal break flag.
   Why:    the tasks are real-format tasks by construction. This is the point of the prior.

13. Task layout
   Rule:   a context of 48-126 rows (drawn per batch), a 5-row gap, 8 question rows.
   Why:    matches the real walk-forward set-up (window 126, gap 5). Varying the length
           makes the model robust to shorter contexts.

14. Scaling
   Rule:   inputs standardised on the context only and clipped at +/-5; a time column from
           0 to 1 across the context and 1 for the questions.
   Why:    exactly as on real data.

15. Efficiency and safety
   Rule:   900-day series, 8 tasks cut from each. A series whose volatility "explodes"
           (non-finite inputs, or above 500% a year) is discarded.
   Why:    speed, and protection against absurd simulated stocks.


C. HOW THE SETTINGS ARE CHOSEN (CALIBRATION)
--------------------------------------------

16. Separate calibration stocks  [NEW in version 2]
   Rule:   calibration uses only the 20 stocks in Data\stocks_calibration (full history).
           The 20 test stocks are never used for calibration, only for the final realism
           check; the script stops if the two folders share a stock.
   Why:    version 1 was calibrated on the same stocks it was tested on. Separate stocks
           make the test leak-free in assets.

17. Ten real-data targets  [two NEW in version 2]
   Rule:   tail heaviness; volatility clustering at 1, 5 and 20 days; the share of "yes"
           answers; answer persistence; break onsets per 126 days; logistic regression AUC;
           and, new, the leverage effect (correlation of today's return with the next 5
           days' average absolute return) and the correlation of the distance from the
           20-day average with the answer.
   Why:    together they cover what decides the task: tails, clustering, breaks,
           overlapping answers, difficulty, and now the asymmetry. None depends on the
           volatility level. The two new targets let the data decide how much asymmetry
           is needed.

18. The score
   Rule:   the sum of squared differences between simulated and real medians, each in units
           of its spread across the real stocks.
   Why:    so that no single statistic dominates.

19. Four stages
   Rule:   stage 1, 180 combinations of clustering and breaks (other settings at middle
           values); stage 2, 60 combinations of tails, onset-shock size and range noise;
           stage 3, 48 combinations of the asymmetry settings (LEVERAGE x SHOCK_DOWN x
           BREAK_DRIFT) [NEW]; stage 4, one exact rescaling of the volatility level.
   Why:    searching everything at once would be too slow. Middle values in stage 1 avoid
           biasing it towards one end.

20. Safeguards
   Rule:   32 simulated series per combination and 40 for the final settings; combinations
           that explode are scored as unrealistic; a warning when the best value sits at
           the edge of the range tried; a stopping rule fixed in advance: at most one
           extension of the grid, and its result is accepted.
           Used once: the first run ended at the edge for breaks per year (5, highest),
           break size (0.15, lowest) and tail degrees of freedom (4, lowest). The grid was
           widened in those directions only (breaks per year 7.5 and 10; break size 0.05
           and 0.1; degrees of freedom 3). Any edge that remains is reported, not extended.
   Why:    to reduce luck, and to stop the tuning from going on indefinitely.

21. Out-of-sample realism check
   Rule:   the final simulated stocks are compared with the 20 test stocks on the same
           statistics (the "distance to test stocks" in Table 4).
   Why:    shows whether the prior matches stocks it was never calibrated on.
